Abstract

Vision now infers geometry and motion at scale. PhySight targets what video does not show: the contact, force, friction, and mass that govern interaction and appear only through their effects. The workshop asks how much of this hidden physics can be inferred from video, as judged by instrument measurements. It compares generalist models with learned inverse models trained on measured touch and force. A challenge scores hand contact inferred from egocentric video against tactile-glove measurements from TouchScale. Models trained with and without touch are ranked separately. The program features papers, invited talks, a live visual-tactile demo, and awards.

What can video reveal? A first question is which hidden quantities the visual evidence determines and which it leaves ambiguous. Each quantity becomes visible through its own event, such as deformation for contact, slip for friction, and collision for mass. When that event is missing, a model should report its uncertainty. An agent can also act to create the event, as in interactive perception. Results that show what video cannot recover are as welcome as new methods.

How can models infer it? A second question is how models should solve this inverse problem, and two routes compete. Generalist models bring physical knowledge from pretraining and simulation. For example, a vision-language agent can rebuild the scene in a physics engine and tune it until the simulation matches the video. A video or world model can also act as the physics engine. Learned inverse models instead map video to hidden physics after training on recordings that pair video with measured touch and force. Scoring both routes against the same measurements shows where each works and how to combine them.

How can measurement verify it? A third question is how to tell whether an inference is right. Instruments such as tactile gloves and force sensors measure the hidden quantities while the camera records. Paired recordings serve as training data for learned inverse models and as ground truth for both routes. The first edition starts with hand contact. Every manipulation begins with contact, and tactile gloves record it at scale.

Call for Papers

We welcome work on physical scene understanding, visual-tactile learning, contact and force estimation, inverse physics, simulation, and evaluation of physical reasoning in generalist models.

We plan to accept long papers of up to 8 pages and short papers of up to 4 pages, excluding references and supplementary material. Submissions will follow a double-blind review process using the CVPR paper format. The track is non-archival, with no CVF proceedings.

In-progress work, negative results, uncertainty analyses, and recently published or encore work are welcome. The OpenReview submission site and template link will be announced after workshop acceptance.

Contact Prediction Challenge

Planned for 2027; subject to workshop acceptance. Dates and protocol follow the current proposal.

The challenge applies the workshop's question to hand contact. Models infer from egocentric video when and where each hand touches something, and a tactile glove provides the ground truth.

Given a clip of head-camera video, predict for every frame the probability that each of the 30 glove pads (15 per hand) is in contact. Methods may use depth from the head camera. Wrist cameras and touch are not provided at test time. We release a map from each pad to regions of the MANO hand mesh, so methods that predict contact on the hand surface can enter.

Two categories

The categories separate the generalist and learned inverse approaches described above. The touch-supervised category may train on tactile signals, as learned inverse models do. The touch-free category is open to generalist models and may use video, depth, hand pose, simulation, pretrained models, and contact labels from geometry or human annotation. It may not use tactile, pressure, or force measurements from any dataset, or models trained on them. Both categories face the same hidden test set and are ranked separately, so the gap between them shows how much measured touch adds.

Evaluation

The primary metric is pad-level contact F1 at a 0.5 threshold over all frames and pads, averaged over Split A and Split B. Secondary metrics are the Brier score of the predicted probabilities, the median error in contact onset time, and the F1 gap between female and male participants in Split B. We also report F1 by hand size. Teams submit to an EvalAI server with a public validation leaderboard and a hidden test leaderboard. Each team may make at most three test submissions. We provide a touch-free baseline that lifts hands to 3D with HaMeR and labels contact by distance to the depth surface. A touch-supervised baseline trains a contact head on the same HaMeR features with glove labels. The organizers also report reference results for public generalist models in the touch-free category. We re-run the code of the top teams before announcing winners. Top teams are invited to co-author the challenge report.

Data and availability

Training and validation use the public TouchScale release on Hugging Face (CC BY-NC 4.0, gated access). To date, the release provides 100 of the planned 500 hours (15,324 episodes, 929 tasks, and 22 scene types), recorded with a head RGB-D camera, two wrist cameras, and tactile gloves with 880 taxels per hand. We will publish official splits, with no participant overlap, on January 15, 2027.

Hidden test set

The test set has two parts, and its labels stay hidden from participants. Split A holds about 10 hours that 2077AI will withhold from every public release, with participants and scenes unseen in training. Split B holds about 5 hours from at least 20 new participants, recorded at Texas A&M from December 2026 to February 2027 with the same gloves and cameras. Half of the Split B participants will be female, and we record each participant's hand size. Each pad has its own contact threshold, set from its no-load noise floor and checked against a reference load cell. A pad is labeled in contact when its reading exceeds this threshold. Glove readings are resampled to the 30 Hz video timeline. Two annotators check 2,000 sampled test frames against the wrist cameras, and we publish their agreement with the glove labels.

Contingency

If Split B is not finished by February 28, 2027 or Split A is not available, the ranking uses the available split alone. Robot-hand recordings with the same glove then form an unranked transfer track.

Data ethics

PhySight works with egocentric recordings of people. TouchScale identifies participants only by pseudonymous IDs and is shared under CC BY-NC 4.0 with gated access. The TouchScale paper states that the data-collection vendor obtained informed consent from participants before recording. Before the challenge opens, we will publish these consent terms, and Split A will use only recordings whose consent covers benchmark use. Split B will be recorded under a Texas A&M IRB protocol with informed consent that covers use in the challenge. Bystander faces in all challenge videos will be blurred before release. The current TouchScale release is predominantly male (95 of its 15,324 episodes come from female participants), so Split B balances female and male participants and the challenge reports the F1 gap between them. Estimating force from video could be misused to monitor workers without consent. The challenge terms prohibit identifying participants, and each team's two-page report states the misuse risks of its method.
